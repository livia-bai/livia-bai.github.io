On the day of the awards, as the sun was dropping into the sea, the Bellhaven fish market was still singing *who counted the votes*.

The scalers sang it. The head-choppers sang it. The kids scrubbing baskets at the trough sang it. To the tune of "The Deep," and more in time than the Chill Club's paid singers had ever managed.

Over the market gate hung a long wooden board in blue paint: CHILL CLUB LICENSED · BELLHAVEN FISH MARKET. Licensed meant that every morning before dawn, the Chill Club hauled two carts of ice up from its cellar, free. It also meant the only song allowed in the market was the number one on the Chart.

Big Gio climbed onto an upturned fish crate. The crate creaked.

"Motion," he said, and pointed at the board. "The first three words. Let's lose them."

A short silence.

"Not picking a fight with anybody," said Gio. "Just. A fish market's a fish market."

"What about the ice?" someone shouted.

"Who decides?"

"We vote."

"Who counts?"

The whole market laughed. When they were done laughing, they all looked at Rani.

She was still in the clothes she'd worn on stage, a few scales still clinging to her cuffs. She wiped her knife twice on her apron.

"I'll count," she said. "Right here. In front of everybody."

<p class="ds-conch-cut">〰</p>

They voted with skewers.

That was Mona's idea, from the grilled-squid stall. She threw every squid she had left on the grill: one each, no charge. Eat it, then drop the skewer in a bucket. The ice bucket on the left meant keep the words. The fish bucket on the right meant lose them.

"One skewer each," said Mona, banging her tongs on the grill. "Anyone eats two, I put *them* on a stick."

The first to vote was Rani's mother, Bea.

Bea had sold fish in this market for twenty-six years and had a voice that could shout a gull off a mast. She finished her squid in three bites and flicked the skewer into the ice bucket. *Clink.*

"No ice, the fish stinks by noon tomorrow," she told everyone. Then she turned to Rani and brought the volume down a notch. "And that Frost sweated today. A man who's sweated holds a grudge."

Rani looked at her.

"You vote your vote," said Rani. "I'll count my count."

Bea snorted. She didn't leave. She dragged a stool over beside the ice bucket and sat down.

<p class="ds-conch-cut">〰</p>

Gio was seventh in line, holding seven skewers.

"One each," said Rani.

"I'm voting for the family. My mum, my wife, my three girls—"

"Where are they?"

"...At home. Eating dinner."

"Then they can come and eat squid."

Gio handed six skewers back to Mona, who stuck them one by one along the edge of the grill for next time. He dropped the last one in the fish bucket, then bent over and peered in to make sure it had reached the bottom.

Behind him was Toto from the lemonade cart. Toto was fifteen, and one of his sales shouts could drown out the whole square, but he hadn't made a sound all afternoon. He put his skewer in the fish bucket, then set a cup of lemonade down by Rani's hand, the glass still crusted with ice.

"Last of the ice," he said, and left so fast he knocked over a crate.

Rani drank the lemonade, every drop. Bea saw it from her stool, said nothing, and shifted the stool a little closer.

<p class="ds-conch-cut">〰</p>

The sun was down to a sliver when Old Matti turned up. He fixed the lifts under the bell tower, and his hat brim was ringed with eaten squid skewers, a dozen at least.

"These count?" He patted his hat.

"ONE EACH!" said the whole market.

Old Matti grinned, pulled the oldest skewer off his brim, its tip gone black, and dropped it in the fish bucket. "The city's rules are two hundred years older than the Chill Club," he said. "So's the fish market." Then he bought a fresh squid, ate it, and stuck the new skewer back in his hat to fill the gap.

The last to come was someone in a pale blue robe.

The market went quiet all at once. Scaling knives stopped in midair.

It was the young assistant who stood beside Master Frost. Sweat all over his head, no fan in his hand. He'd spent the whole afternoon behind a second-floor window of the Chill Club across the street, and everybody had seen him.

"I'd... like a squid, please."

Mona looked at him for a while, then handed him one. He ate it standing up. Then he stood in front of the two buckets for a long time, holding the skewer, before he dropped it in the fish bucket, wiped his face with his sleeve, and walked off. He didn't say "Stay chill."

Bea watched him go. "That boy started sweating before his boss did."

<p class="ds-conch-cut">〰</p>

Rani counted on her knees on the wet stones.

She tipped both buckets out and straightened the skewers one at a time. Ten to a bundle, three turns of fish twine, a knot. She did this every day. Fish went out on strings of ten too.

Somewhere along the way she started singing. It was still the tune of "Scales," only *scrape, slit, rinse* had turned into *straighten, ten, tie*. The people around her clapped along, one clap a bundle. Toto stood on a crate and called out every bundle as she finished it. He had his voice back. The whole harbour could hear him.

Bea had been sitting. Then she was crouching. Then she was straightening skewers too. The tips were sticky with squid sauce, so after each one she wiped her hand on her apron.

The stack of bundles on the right stood a good deal taller than the one on the left.

"Keep: ninety-seven," said Rani, getting up with two wet patches on her knees. "Lose: two hundred and eighty-nine."

Nobody cheered. People looked at each other, slightly embarrassed to have won.

"Ninety-seven," said Bea. "That's not nothing. Everybody in early tomorrow. No ice now, so the fish has to be gone by noon."

<p class="ds-conch-cut">〰</p>

Gio fetched a ladder, took the board down, and sawed it in half. CHILL CLUB LICENSED on one piece. BELLHAVEN FISH MARKET on the other.

The second piece went back over the gate, crooked. Everyone agreed crooked was fine.

Bea claimed the first piece. She flipped it blue side down and laid it on her stall. "Good timber," she said. "Chopping board."

Before the market closed that day, Rani sang "Scales" at the gate. No stage, no conch. Her voice was small and her long notes wobbled. When she got to the part where she counts the fish, she counted one, and the whole market counted one with her, the ninety-seven who'd voted for the ice bucket included.

The loudest was Bea. She counted, and on her new chopping board she took the head off a fish.
