In 2021 the Australian Open got rid of every line judge. The system that replaced Helen called the lines in Helen's voice.

She kept it to herself for five years. She agreed to talk on two conditions: no real name, and no mention of which hospital. She picked "Helen" herself. There are too many Helens among line judges, she says. Nobody will narrow it down.

She is fifty-nine and has spent thirty years in the intensive care unit of a hospital on Melbourne's north side. Starting in 1999, every January, she took two weeks of annual leave and went to Melbourne Park to sit behind a baseline. Twenty-two tournaments.

In December 2020 she got an email. Next year, Hawk-Eye Live would call the lines on every court, a first for a Grand Slam; her services would not be required this year; thank you for your many years of support. Under the sign-off was a smiley face.

Three weeks later, the hospital admin office sent a notice round. The Open wanted to honour frontline workers: a few nurses, cleaners and delivery drivers were invited to record line calls, to be played by the system during matches. Lunch break, staff car park, a white van.

"I went," she says. "I wanted to see who was replacing me."

Inside: a folding chair, a microphone, a young man in headphones. Three words. *Out. Fault. Foot fault.* She sat down and called the first *Out*, and her right arm went out on its own, palm flat, pointing at the wall of the van.

The engineer looked up at her.

"Once more," he said. "Brighter. Younger."

She called it again, the way she'd called it on court at twenty-five.

"You've done this before?"

"No."

* * *

On 13 February, Victoria went into a snap lockdown. The tennis went on; the stands were emptied. John Cain Arena, ten thousand seats, not one person in them.

Kieran Ashby, thirty-four, a local wildcard, was playing the third round of his last Australian Open. Helen knew him. In 2009, on the same court — it was called Hisense Arena then — a twenty-two-year-old Ashby hit a ball near the baseline. She called it out. He challenged. Hawk-Eye: on the line, by one millimetre. He shouted at her to get her eyes tested. It was the only time a machine ever overruled her. For twelve years after that she called something like forty of his matches. They nodded when they passed. They didn't speak.

That day she was at home before a night shift, with the TV on. Fourth-set tiebreak, Ashby down five–six. A backhand down the line, dropping right onto the baseline.

"OUT!"

In the empty arena the call rang very clear. Bright, young, with a tiny catch before the T.

Under this year's rules he had no challenge to use. He didn't look up at the big screen. He walked behind the baseline, to where a line judge should have been sitting and where there was now only an empty chair. He stood in front of it for two seconds, and the courtside microphone carried what he said to the whole country, live:

"Helen?"

* * *

He lost the match. In the press conference someone asked who Helen was. He said he'd been joking.

The commentators never worked it out. The line judges did.

They had a group chat, two hundred-odd people, called Line Up, silent since December. At seven the next morning Deb posted the clip: "That's Helen's Out. That catch before the T — I've heard it for twenty years."

Sixty-odd messages by lunch. Someone said Deb was hearing things. Someone went looking for a list of the frontline workers and couldn't find one. Someone wrote: "You can boo a line judge. You can't boo a nurse."

In the afternoon somebody typed one word: *scab*. Helen left the group.

Two days later a communications officer from the tournament rang her. He didn't say whether the voice was hers. He only asked that if any media got in touch, she pass them on to him, "to protect the privacy of all our frontline workers." He was polite, she says. Polite like someone checking a medication chart.

When she hung up, she printed the hospital notice, the van's parking registration and her own roster for that day, and locked them in a drawer. "In case one day someone says I went looking for it."

Ashby retired that autumn. Later, on a podcast, the host asked him about "Helen" that night.

"Helen who?" he said.

We contacted the company that did the recordings. They replied that the retention and use of the material is a commercial arrangement and they were unable to comment. The engineer has since left. We found him online, and he sent back one message: everyone recorded each word twice, and they picked whichever take "sounded most like a decision." He said he couldn't place a nurse.

We asked the tournament whether those recordings were used in the years after. At the time of publication, they had not replied.
