The lift stopped halfway down at exactly noon.

"Inspection hour," said Old Matti. He threw the brake and fished a cold squid skewer out of his tool bag.

The lift was an iron cage hanging in the shaft under the bell tower, on four cables, driven by the waterwheel on the east side of the square. Twenty feet over our heads were the paving stones, with a few blades of light and some grill smoke leaking through the cracks. Below us, dark, and a cold salty draught that came up in breaths.

I was in the middle of the cage, lying in a crate of rotten fishing net, mouth to the sky. Nobody had bothered with the velvet cap. They didn't think they'd need it.

"Master Matti," said Fenn, "the President said before noon."

Fenn was the young assistant. Yesterday he didn't have a name. This morning, down in the Chill Club cellar, Master Frost yelled "Fenn!" at him eleven times. I counted. He was still in his pale blue robe, he'd forgotten his fan, and he was sweating through his hair.

"Before noon," said Old Matti, chewing. "It's noon."

"So..."

"One hour's inspection. City rule. Two hundred years older than your Chill Club." He stuck the bare skewer into his hatband, next to a row of others. "What's the rush? It's not going anywhere."

He nodded at me with his chin.

He had a point. I was not going anywhere. That morning in the cellar Master Frost had passed sentence: *Faulty equipment. Return it to the deep.* He thought I couldn't hear him from inside the crate. The deep meant the bottom of this shaft.

Fenn took half a step back, bumped the bars, and the cage swung. A blade of light swung with it, down the shaft, across the bottom—

The bottom wasn't a bottom.

It was water, and on the water there was a city. A stone bridge with an iron railing and a life ring hung on the rail. A tall ship lying on its side, its hull leaned up against a row of pointed roofs, its masts stabbing crooked into the dark. Mooring posts tilting out of the water. The light swung back, and the city was gone.

Under Bellhaven, there was another Bellhaven.

When he'd finished the squid, Old Matti got to work. Work, in his case, meant pressing his ear to a cable and not moving, like a man listening to the neighbours fight through a wall.

"What are you listening for?" Fenn asked.

"For it to sing. A cable's strands twisted together. Good cable, pulled tight, goes *hmmm*. One note. When a strand's about to go, you get a second note."

I don't have ears. I *am* an ear.

He was right. Three of the cables sang the same low note, thick and steady, like an open string on a cello. The back-left one had something hiding under the low note. A thin sound. *Ting.* A pause. *Ting.* Finer than a pigeon's claw on a tin roof.

That was steel wire, snapping one hair at a time.

Eleven years of live sound. I can pick a dodgy connection out of thirty thousand people screaming. Also, I have a personal grudge against cables.

Old Matti moved from one to the next. "Fine." "Fine." "Fine." He got to the back-left one just as somebody up in the square started shouting about iced lemonade, and the shout poured down through the cracks and buried the *ting* completely.

"Fine," he said.

It was not fine.

I tried to yell. What came out was surf. *Hhhhaaaa*— Fenn flinched, the cage swung again, and the back-left cable went *ting* twice.

Old Matti opened one eye, looked at me, and shut it. "That thing makes noises?"

"The President says it's faulty."

"Mm. Then it's faulty." Somehow, in an iron cage, he managed to roll over. He settled against the bars and tipped his hat down. "Half an hour left. I'm resting my eyes."

<p class="ds-conch-cut">〰</p>

I lay in the netting and thought.

I can't talk. All I can do is play back what I've heard. Rani's "Scales." Ten thousand people singing *who counted the votes*. Master Frost saying *return it to the deep*. Rats in the cellar. And those two *tings*.

In my old trade there's a move called soloing. You take one thing out of the whole pot of sound, mute everything else, and push it all the way up.

I muted the lemonade. Muted the waterwheel. Muted Fenn's breathing and Old Matti's snoring. Took those two *tings*, and pushed the fader to the top.

<p class="ds-conch-ding">TING!<br>TING!</p>

Like an iron spoon on an iron pot. It went up and down the shaft twice, and then came back up off the water at the bottom for a third.

Old Matti's hat fell off. Skewers everywhere.

He didn't look at me. He looked at the back-left cable. For a long time. Then he crawled over and put his ear to it, and this time he held it there so long that Fenn stopped breathing too.

*Ting.*

"Seventh strand," said Old Matti. He took a stub of chalk from his bag and drew a ring around the cable. "Two more trips down and it lets go."

Fenn went white. "So if we'd kept going just now—"

"Then three of us get returned to the deep." Old Matti turned his head and looked at me properly for the first time. "Three."

He lifted me out of the netting, turned me over and knocked on my shell, like a man picking a watermelon.

"You in the trade?"

I can't nod. I played him one *ting*.

He grinned. Two teeth missing.

<p class="ds-conch-cut">〰</p>

From the bottom of his bag Old Matti dug out a wooden sign that said OUT OF SERVICE and held it out to Fenn.

Fenn took it and stood there. He wound the string once around his hand. Then again.

"The President..."

"Tell him the lift's broken and we couldn't get down," said Old Matti. "It is broken. You won't even be lying."

Fenn hung the sign on the outside of the gate. Then he sat down on the edge of the crate, robe in the dust, and didn't care.

"There's forty-one more cables in the shafts under this city," Old Matti said, settling me on his knee. "Thirty years of one man's ears. They're going. From now on you listen with me."

Then he leaned back against the bars and held me up to his ear, the way a kid does with a shell they've found on a beach.

"Twenty minutes left of inspection hour."

He wanted to hear the sea. What I played him wasn't the sea. It was a bit from the cellar that morning: Master Frost talking something over with someone, his voice kept very low. He said "Rani." He said something about a ship.

Old Matti's snoring stopped.

He didn't open his eyes. He just pressed me a little tighter to his ear.

"From the top," he said. "Again."
