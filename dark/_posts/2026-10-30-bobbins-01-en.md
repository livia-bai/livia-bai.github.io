The man in the gray coat is back today, and this time he brought horses. Three.

Somebody has to tell her. But there's nobody in this booth except her and twenty-four bobbins, and I'm one of the bobbins.

<p class="ds-bob-lace" aria-hidden="true"></p>

I'm number twenty-four. Pine. Whittled four months ago in the back of a wagon in Kansas to replace one that broke. The other twenty-three came from Venice, bone and boxwood, older than Buzzard Fork, older than this whole country, and they know with their eyes shut whether the next move is a cross or a twist. I don't. I have to watch. And I hang off the far left of the pillow, closest to the window, so I have to watch outside too. I didn't pick that.

Buzzard Fork has one crossing. Main Street runs east–west; the ore road comes screaming down from the Hollow Tooth Mine, north–south. The ore carts can't brake on that grade. Last year they hit two horses, an ice man, and a piano. So they put up a pole at the crossing with a board on it, red on one side, green on the other. At the foot of the pole they nailed up this booth, and in the booth sits Mrs. Ottavia with a lace pillow on her knees.

She doesn't own a watch. She keeps time in lace.

What we're making is an insertion two fingers wide: two straight edges, and between them one diamond after another. She calls each diamond an "eye." Ore road green, seven eyes. All red, two eyes. Main Street green, nine eyes. All red again, two. All red means everybody stops, so whatever's still stuck in the crossing can clear out.

The numbers were her husband's. Ettore was the mine's engineer, and he finished the drawings and then died of a fever. Before he went, he took her pattern card and put a dot of red ink beside every pinhole where the board should flip. On the back of the card he wrote one line:

*Don't look at the street. If you look, you'll want to help, and helping is how it goes wrong.*

So she doesn't look at the street. And the ore drivers don't look at her. They look at the board. That's the whole thing. That's what holds it up.

<p class="ds-bob-lace" aria-hidden="true"></p>

Gray Coat has been sitting on the bench outside the feed store for three days. Every time the board flips, he clicks his pocket watch and writes something in a little book. Day one I figured he was waiting for someone. Day two I figured he was waiting for the ore carts. Day three I got it: he's timing us.

Today there are three horses beside him, reins not tied, just looped once over the rail, all of them facing downhill. And two more people: a tall woman in a man's coat, and a kid hugging two empty flour sacks.

Across the street is the bank.

"Old Bone," I say. "Old Bone, Old Bone, Old Bone—"

"Cross," says Old Bone. He's my partner, a cow bone gone yellow nearly to black. I cross. "Twist." I twist.

"They're going to rob the bank!"

"Third pin on the left. Don't skip it."

<p class="ds-bob-lace" aria-hidden="true"></p>

The ore road is green, and the carts are coming down off the mountain one behind another, twelve of them, iron wheels shaking the whole crossing so hard the pins in the pillow hum. She hums along. Her eyes stay on the pins.

The more I spin, the more I understand, and the more I understand, the sicker I feel. Nine eyes of Main Street green: enough to ride from the bank to the crossing. Then all red. Then twelve ore carts come down and cut Main Street clean in half, and anyone chasing is shut out on the far side.

A red dot slides by under my belly. She doesn't look up. Her hand finds the cord on its own and pulls, and the board flips to red. Gray Coat puts his watch in his pocket and stands.

"I'll fall off!" I say. "The thread tangles, she has to stop—"

"She stops, and Main Street stays green," says Old Bone. "You'd be holding the door for them."

"Then I'll make her go faster!"

"Faster, and the board flips early. The drivers only look at the board. When that first cart comes down, who's still standing in the crossing?"

I don't know who's still standing in the crossing. All I know is the sap is about to come out of me.

The next red dot arrives. The board flips green.

<p class="ds-bob-lace" aria-hidden="true"></p>

Gray Coat and the tall woman go into the bank. The kid stays outside, three sets of reins in one fist.

They're in. They're *in!* Does nobody see this? A woman with a basket of eggs walks past the door, glances inside, keeps walking.

Halfway through a diamond, the two of them come out, a sack on each shoulder. The tall woman's sack has a hole in the bottom, and it leaks silver dollars all down the boardwalk, bouncing.

And then I slip.

Not on purpose, I swear. I spin half a turn too far on my thread and go right over the edge of the pillow, swinging in midair, and I see the floorboards, and I see her shoe, a little embroidered flower on the toe worn almost bald.

Her hand comes down. It doesn't pause. She doesn't look. Two fingers pinch me, wind the thread back up, set me down beside Old Bone, and while they're there they undo the last stitch and make it again. Her humming doesn't miss a note.

The horses go. All three, down the slope toward the crossing, the kid flat along the middle one's neck. Someone runs out of the bank after them, and the first shot goes into the sky.

When they tear through the crossing, they're less than twelve feet from me. Gray Coat, passing the window, lifts his hat toward the booth. Not toward me. Toward her.

She doesn't look up. The red dot arrives; she pulls the cord. All red.

<p class="ds-bob-lace" aria-hidden="true"></p>

During the all red, Sheriff Halloran rides up with four men and reins in hard under the red board. He obeys that board. The whole town does. He's the one who pounded the table for it at the town council.

"Flip it!" he yells at the booth. "Flip it over!"

Then the ore carts come. Twelve of them, nose to tail, iron wheels and the stamp mill's hammers up the mountain all slamming at once, and it buries everything. The sheriff's mouth is moving. Not a word gets in. Mrs. Ottavia hums. Cross, twist, cross.

The carts pass. The dust settles. Down Main Street there's nothing left, only a line of silver dollars running from the bank door to the crossing, one bright spot after another. To me it looks exactly like a pricked pattern card.

The sheriff doesn't give chase. He sits on his horse and looks at the booth for a long time.

<p class="ds-bob-lace" aria-hidden="true"></p>

At seven o'clock she finishes the last diamond, pins off the thread, and lays the cloth over the pillow.

"Very tidy today," she tells us.

Before the cloth comes down, I see the sheriff's deputy carry a chair over and set it beside the bench outside the feed store. He sits, opens a pocket watch, and rests it on his knee.
