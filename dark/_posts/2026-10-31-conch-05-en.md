The cage lurched towards the back-left corner, and I rolled out of the netting into the corner of the crate.

Another strand had gone in the back-left cable. The cage dropped a finger's width, caught, and all four cables hummed for a long time.

Old Matti didn't listen to the cable. He looked up at the twenty feet over our heads. The light leaking through the cracks in the flagstones had tilted too.

"Can't wait out the hour," he said. "Somebody has to get up there and tell the east wheel to haul us. Slower than a snail. One yank and that one goes."

Fenn looked at him. Then at the cable.

"These knees won't do twenty feet," said Old Matti. "You take the front-right. That one sings the steadiest."

Fenn started sweating. I'd known him two days, and in two days he'd sweated more than the fish market sluices off its floor in a morning.

Old Matti lifted me out of the crate and weighed me in his hand.

"You going up with him, or staying here with me?"

He was asking me.

If I stayed with Old Matti, I could keep an ear on that cable and give him a *ting* before it went. And then what? A *ting* doesn't catch an iron cage.

If I went up with Fenn, up was the square, and past the square was the fish market, and in the fish market was Rani. Someone was planning to put her inside a ship lying on a rooftop, and she didn't know.

As for Fenn: a little while ago, after he'd hung the OUT OF SERVICE sign and come back to sit down, he'd let out a breath. The sinking kind, chest all the way down to belly. People only breathe like that when they've just put something heavy down. Eleven years of listening to people breathe. I've never once got it wrong.

I played a *ting*.

"He's Chill Club," said Old Matti.

*Ting.*

Old Matti looked at me for a good while. Then he undid his belt and strapped me to Fenn's chest, mouth up.

"Fine. Your ears are better than mine."

Fenn grabbed the front-right cable and kicked off from the crossbar on the cage roof. The cage swung the other way. Back-left: *ting*.

"Don't look down," said Old Matti.

Fenn looked down. So did I. The water at the bottom was black and shiny, and the ship lay on its side across the rooftops, masts stabbing crooked into the dark.

He climbed. His robe tangled round his legs, so he bit the hem and held it in his teeth. His palms sweated the cable to a shine; he slid half a foot, and caught himself. I was pressed against his chest with his heartbeat right under my shell, too ragged to mix.

Halfway up, he hung there and couldn't get his breath.

I went through my stock, picked a line, and pushed it up:

<p class="ds-conch-play">"What's the rush? It's not going anywhere."</p>

Old Matti's voice, from this noon, with squid on its breath.

Fenn snorted through his nose. Nearly laughed. The breath came back.

Near the top, he started talking, muffled in his robe.

"I joined the Club when I was twelve. Before that I hauled ice on the docks. Froze a fingernail clean off. The Master took me in. Gave me a robe. Gave me a fan. He said, Fenn, you're good at everything except you sweat."

His hand closed on the iron grating at the mouth of the shaft.

"Six years I've been practising. Still sweat."

<p class="ds-conch-cut">〰</p>

The grating pushed open easy. Outside was the patch of shade at the foot of the bell tower, the noon sun cutting the shadow into a neat square.

In the shade there was a little table. White cloth, one corner hanging down to the flagstones. A yellow bowl, empty. A dark brown glazed jug, beaded with water, a chip knocked out of the lip so the white showed through. A white plate with a little heap of apples, half red, half yellow; one had rolled off the plate and come to rest on the cloth, next to a small knife. Behind the table stood a green screen crawling with leaves, keeping the smoke from the square off the food.

Master Frost sat at the table with his fan across his knee.

He'd been having his lunch here. Waiting for the splash.

"Fenn," he said. "Is it down?"

Fenn knelt at the mouth of the shaft, soaked through, with me strapped to his chest.

I waited for him to lie. Old Matti had just taught him how. Say the lift broke. It is broken. You won't even be lying.

"The lift needs hauling slow," said Fenn. "Old Matti's still in it. The back-left cable's about to go."

"Mm," said Master Frost. "And the thing?"

Fenn looked down at me. Then he undid the belt, lifted me in both hands, and set me on the white cloth, next to the plate.

"Get the wheel going," he said. "Slow."

Master Frost pointed his fan towards the east side of the square. Under a tree, a pale blue robe stood up and trotted away.

I had one card left. The foot of the bell tower is the best-sounding spot in the city, and there were a few hundred people eating lunch in the square. The cellar recording, Frost planning to put Rani in the ship: played straight, it would be enough.

I pushed it all the way up.

<p class="ds-conch-play">"The fish girl. That voice is the re<span class="ds-conch-sink">— ○ ° o</span></p>

Master Frost's hand was quicker. He picked me up, dropped me in the yellow bowl, lifted the brown jug and poured. Ice water. Chill Club ice, from the cellar.

The rest of the line came out as bubbles, *glub glub*, popping at the rim. Nobody heard a thing.

I played it again. Bubbles. *Ting.* Bubbles.

You can't play sound out of water. But you can hear better in water than anywhere. I knew that in my last life. Today's the first time I've tested it in person.

I heard Master Frost pick up the apple that had rolled off the plate and peel it with the little knife, one long strip, unbroken.

I heard him hold it out. "You've sweated so much. Eat."

I heard Fenn take it.

"Stay chill," said Master Frost.

A long time later, Fenn said, "...Stay chill."

<p class="ds-conch-cut">〰</p>

The east wheel started. Through the water I could hear every wooden tooth bite in: *clok*, a long wait, *clok*. Slower than a snail, honestly.

All four cables began to draw up. Three sang the low note. The back-left one gave a *ting* under it, then another, and then nothing. The cage came up flush with the shaft.

Old Matti climbed out through the grating, a few skewers short in his hatband. He straightened up and looked at the table first. At the yellow bowl. At me, soaking in it. Then at the peeled white apple in Fenn's hand.

Fenn hadn't taken a bite. The apple in his hand was going brown.

Old Matti looked for a long time.

"Fine," he said.

The last time he said that word, it was to a cable that was about to snap.
