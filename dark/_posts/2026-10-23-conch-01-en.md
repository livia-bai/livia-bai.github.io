I died because of one cable nobody taped down.

Eleven years as a live sound engineer. Outdoor show, thirty thousand people. I was hauling a monitor wedge from stage left to stage right, caught my foot, and met the corner of the mixing desk with my skull. Last thing I heard was the singer yelling, "Thank you, everybody—"

Fair enough. At least it was an outro.

When I came to, someone was blowing into my mouth.

"Testing. Testing."

A thick, nasal voice poured in at one end of me, looped around three times, and blasted out the other end — into a ridiculously large public square. Ten thousand heads turned at once.

I tried to swear. What came out was the sound of surf.

It took me most of the morning to work it out. I was a conch. Waist-high, mouth facing out, mounted on a brass stand in the middle of the stage in the central square of Bellhaven. Speak into me, and the whole city hears you.

I was a microphone.

And today was the annual awards for the Chill Club Chart.

<p class="ds-conch-cut">〰</p>

Chill Club members wear pale blue robes and carry a fan each. Backstage they don't say hello; they say "Stay chill." They run every song in the city: which ones can be sung in taverns, which ones on the docks, which ones only behind closed doors. It all comes down to the Chart. Once a year, the number one song becomes the song the whole city sings for the next twelve months.

Over ten thousand people were packed into the square. Grilled-squid stalls, iced-lemonade carts, kids riding on shoulders, kids hanging off the bell tower railings. Whistles. Banners with song titles painted on bedsheets. A big man shoved his way to the edge of the stage and bellowed at me: "Hey! Shell! This year you let the fish girl win!"

I wanted to tell him: buddy, I'm equipment.

Before the show, somebody fitted me with a velvet cap. Mouth covered, sound trapped: muted. But I could still hear. Anyone who's worked live knows this. The deadliest thing anyone ever says is said while they think the mic is off.

"Is everything arranged?" The nasal voice. Master Frost, president of the Chill Club. Through the velvet I could see his fan going at an unhurried pace and his forehead perfectly dry. The whole city was sweating. Not him.

"It's arranged," said a young assistant. "Only, sir... in the ballot boxes, the fishmonger, Rani, she's got the most votes. About three times the runner-up."

"And who counted the votes?"

"...You did."

"Then 'The Deep' is number one." The fan paused for one beat. "After the award, have the whole square sing the chorus. Ten thousand voices, one line, not a syllable wrong. *Bell of the deep, awaken.* Three times through, and the door underneath opens."

"And after the door opens?"

Master Frost gave a short laugh. "After the door opens, we won't need to bother with a Chart."

Under the velvet, I shivered. When a conch shivers, it sounds like a ship going down somewhere far off.

<p class="ds-conch-cut">〰</p>

Rani was the third act.

Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Fish scales still stuck to her cuffs, a fresh knife cut across the back of her hand. She walked up and grabbed my brass stand so hard it creaked.

"I'll, um, just check it," she whispered to me. "Testing?"

I caught that "testing" and spun it once through my insides.

Occupational hazard.

Under her voice sat a low hum: the bellows on the row of grills on the east side of the square. I cut it. Her top notes were a bit sharp; I pulled that band down. She ran short on breath, so I slipped a little echo under the tail of each line, like the hiss of sand after a wave goes back out.

She sang "Scales." A song about gutting fish, literally: scrape, slit, rinse, counting out loud how many she'd sold today.

By the second verse the square had gone quiet. By the chorus, ten thousand people were stamping. Every pigeon on the bell tower took off.

The big man was crying like a sea cucumber fresh out of the water.

When Rani finished, she stared at me as if to ask: was that me?

It was you. All I did was push what was already in you up to the front of the mix.

<p class="ds-conch-cut">〰</p>

The award. Master Frost stepped up and unrolled a gold-edged scroll.

"This year's Chill Club Chart, number one—" A pause. A professional pause. "—'The Deep'!"

Scattered applause, and a lot more "...huh?"

"Please join me," Master Frost said, lifting his fan, "in the chorus! *Bell of the deep*—"

A few plants in the front row started singing. The people around them hesitated, mouths half open.

I thought: all right. I'm a conch.

When you were little and held a seashell to your ear, you heard the ocean, didn't you? The grown-ups said that's because it keeps the sea inside.

They were only half right. What a conch keeps inside is everything it's ever heard.

So I took the line I'd heard under the velvet cap and let it out exactly as it was:

<p class="ds-conch-play">"And who counted the votes?"<br>"...You did."</p>

Ten thousand mouths shut at the same time.

Master Frost's fan stopped mid-air. I saw it, plain as anything: one bead of sweat, rolling down a forehead that had been dry its entire life.

"Th-that's—" He spun to face the crowd. "That's the new version of 'The Deep'! New lyrics! Come on, everyone—"

The big man at the edge of the stage got it first. He cleared his throat and, to the tune of "The Deep," let rip:

"WHO — COUNTED — THE — VOTES —"

The people next to him came in: "YOU — DID!"

Then the whole square. Ten thousand voices, one line, not a syllable wrong. Three times through.

No door opened underneath us.

Something else opened instead: a laugh that started at the squid stall and rolled all the way up the bell tower. People doubled over on the cobbles. Somebody sprayed lemonade down the neck of the man in front. A kid on the tower railing nearly fell off. Rani stood at the side of the stage with both hands over her mouth, crying from laughing.

Master Frost turned around and looked straight at me. The fan snapped shut. *Clack.*

I knew I was in serious trouble.

I couldn't help it. A conch laughing out loud sounds like a high tide at noon, and it poured out of me and flooded the whole square.
