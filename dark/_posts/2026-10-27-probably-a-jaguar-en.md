### Kit

"GOOOOLD!"

Merv the guard came up out of his chair and slapped the front desk. On the little TV it was the Nagano Paralympics, and an Australian had just crossed the line in the downhill.

Under that roar, Kit pulled open drawer seven. Drawer seven shrieked like a stepped-on cat. Nobody heard.

Dot had watched Merv for three weeks: the minute the racing started in Nagano, the TV went up on the front desk and he wouldn't have looked up for the Pope. Kit came down through the storeroom skylight — touchdown, drawer, grab, hauled back up to the roof on the rope — forty seconds. There was a yellowed index card underneath the thing. She didn't have time to read it.

Two streets on, Dot finally laid it flat on her palm: pale green jade, a hand long, bloomed with patches the colour of rust, and stretched along it, some kind of animal. The typed label on the drawer said JAGUAR PENDANT.

"Done," said Dot.

Rennie put his foot down. In the back, Sione cried. He always cried when he was happy.

### Dot

Abernathy looked at it through a loupe for five minutes in the back room of the laundromat.

"Where are the spots?"

"What spots?"

"Jaguars have spots. This has —" he tapped the spirals with a fingernail "— curly bits. I'd say that's a dog."

"Label says jaguar."

"My client runs a Jaguar dealership. He's got two hundred and twelve jaguars at home. China, bronze, stuffed. The toilet seat's got rosettes. He wants jaguar number two hundred and thirteen, not dog number one." He slid it back across the table. "Find out what it is. Then come and see me."

Forty thousand dollars, gone, on account of a rock. Dot put it back in her pocket, and for the first time, she actually looked at it.

### Sione

Sione spent an afternoon in front of the jaguar enclosure at the zoo.

The jaguar was asleep on a rock shelf, stretched out flat, head to the right, tail curled once round at the left. Sione held up the jade and compared. Head to the right. Tail curled once round. Ear: a little whirlpool. Mouth: one flat line.

A keeper walked past. Sione stopped him with one enormous hand. "A jaguar's eye. Is the pupil round, or a slit?"

"Round. Slits are house cats."

Sione looked down at the jade eye. A circle. As round as round gets.

Back in the van, his eyes were wet again. "It's having a nap."

### Kit

Kit spent a day in the State Library and came back with eleven pages of notes.

She was talking before the van door was open. "The Maya thought jade was worth more than gold! Green was corn, it was water, it was *breath* — the breath that comes out of you! When they drew people talking, they drew a little curl coming out of the mouth!"

"So?"

"So these curls aren't decoration! It's breathing! It's *talking*!" She jabbed at the rust-coloured patches. "And this — the book says when jade's been buried a long time, iron from the soil soaks into it."

Sione slapped his knee. "Then those are spots! The ground gave it spots!"

### Rennie

Rennie hadn't said a word. The day it was his turn to take the jade home, his daughter Ella, who was seven, measured it with the ruler from her pencil case. Twelve point six centimetres. Then she announced:

"Crocodile."

"Why?"

"The mouth's long and it's flat. And it's lying down." She thought about it. "Crocodiles breathe too."

The next day, the van went to war.

"Jaguar! A napping jaguar!"

"A *talking* jaguar!"

"Crocodile. Ella said."

"Ella's seven!"

"So what if she's seven?" For the first time in living memory, Rennie took his hands off the wheel. "Seven-year-olds see things properly!"

### Dot

On day five, Dot rang the museum from a phone box and said she was a postgraduate writing a thesis.

The registrar was delighted. "The jaguar pendant? Drawer seven. All we've got is an index card, handwritten when it came in, in 1953. It's in the drawer with it. Honestly, I've been here fourteen years and I've never once opened drawer seven."

Dot got back in the van. Three heads turned to her at once.

"We go back," she said. "Put it back, and swap it for something in the same drawer that looks like a jaguar. Bound to be one that looks like a jaguar."

It was perfectly reasonable. The other three nodded, each for reasons of their own.

### Merv

Merv had minded this door for eleven years, and in the whole building, only drawer seven made a sound.

That day it was the same Australian on the TV, slalom this time, third place. Merv stood up. "BRON—" he began, and halfway through it struck him that you don't yell like that for a bronze, and he sat back down.

Which is why he heard the cat.

He took his torch into the storeroom. Four people were jammed in front of drawer seven, heads touching: a big bloke, an old lady, a girl as thin as a rope, and a man clutching car keys. Nobody ran. The jade was back where it belonged. Nothing else in the drawer had been touched.

The girl was holding the index card, reading aloud.

"March 1953. Jade. One animal, recumbent." She paused. "Jaguar? Dog? Crocodile? To be looked into."

"So who typed the label?" asked the old lady.

"Not whoever wrote this."

Merv pressed his radio and called the police. Then he leaned in as well, bent down, and had a good long look at the thing in the drawer. All week, he had watched nothing but people hurtling down mountains.

"I reckon," he said, "it's a downhill skier. Tucked. Going for it."

"Then what are the curls?"

"Snow."

When the police walked in, the five of them had argued their way down to the curl on the tail.
