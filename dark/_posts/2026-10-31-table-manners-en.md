<div class="poem">The day I was dismissed, I sang the last verse myself.
Not one rhyme was off. I would like that on the record.</div>

<p class="ds-tm-cut" aria-hidden="true"><span></span><span></span><span></span></p>

<div class="poem">I wrote the song.
Three years of Latin in Augsburg,
and I came to a Count whose money was salt
to teach manners: sit up, no hands,
no bones to the dog,
and how to hold a fork. His Lordship used his to scratch his back.

The house had one gold set, knife, fork, spoon, locked in a cabinet.
For lessons, I could borrow the key whenever I liked.

Martinmas. Fourteen guests.
Having the children sing my song after dinner was my idea.</div>

<div class="poem ds-tm-song"><span class="ds-tm-who">Fritz, ten, holding up the fork</span>Little fork has teeth of four,
pin the quail and nothing more.
Cold quail's kind, it doesn't bite,
one quick gulp and out of sight.</div>

<div class="poem">The cook, in the doorway, put down her ladle.
Last month she lost a cold quail and turned out the kitchen girl, Grete.

"Cold" is for the meter. "Hot" sits wrong on the beat. Amateurs don't hear these things.</div>

<div class="poem ds-tm-song"><span class="ds-tm-who">Luise, eight, holding up the spoon</span>Little spoon with belly of gold,
outside in, the way you're told.
Almond cake with one corner gone?
Turn it round. Now carry on.</div>

<div class="poem">The Countess turned the almond cake halfway round.
One corner gone.

That was presentation. The good side faces the guest.</div>

<div class="poem ds-tm-song"><span class="ds-tm-who">Both together</span>Pour the wine up to the line,
one drop past is not refined.
Take a sip and never tell,
top it up from the kitchen well.</div>

<div class="poem">The butler lifted the decanter to a candle.
This autumn His Lordship said the wine was thin and changed merchants three times.

Moderation.</div>

<div class="poem ds-tm-song"><span class="ds-tm-who">Karl, six, holding up the knife</span>Little knife, your head is round,
never point it, cut things down.
Haunch of deer: the third slice, see —</div>

<div class="poem">The line I wrote next was "goes to His Lordship, not to me."

What Karl sang was:</div>

<div class="poem ds-tm-song ds-tm-off">That's how Teacher cuts it when it's dark!</div>

<div class="poem">He comes downstairs for water when he has bad dreams.

Fourteen guests, and not one of them chewing.

Karl waved the knife at me. Round-tipped.
Never point it at a person. I taught him that.</div>

<p class="ds-tm-cut" aria-hidden="true"><span></span><span></span><span></span></p>

<div class="poem">The Count drew a sheet of paper out of the lesson book.
"One more verse. The children haven't learned it. If you would, Teacher."

I sang it.</div>

<div class="poem ds-tm-song ds-tm-mine">Gold won't tell, just rub it bright;
steel's the tattletale. That's right:
by the stove you dry the blade,
put it back where it was laid.</div>

<div class="poem">The Count set the knife in front of me.
Gold can't cut meat, so the blade has to be steel.
On the blade, right where it meets the handle, a brown spot of rust
sits exactly over the cutler's mark.
The little helmeted man on the handle has his face turned away from me.

The night Karl came down, I'd just washed it.
I slid it up my sleeve and never dried it.</div>

<p class="ds-tm-cut" aria-hidden="true"><span></span><span></span><span></span></p>

<div class="poem">Now I'm at an inn in Ulm, revising the song.
The next house is in cloth.

Quail becomes pear.
The wine verse goes.
Venison becomes bread.

The steel-blade verse stays.
That one's true. And it's well written.</div>
