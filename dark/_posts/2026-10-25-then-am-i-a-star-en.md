<div class="poem">Yes!
Don't stop — I'll tell you while we run.

The sun's falling down behind you.
Don't look back. Let it fall.
Up ahead the sky's gone all the way purple,
and that's where the stars are.

Listen:
the star an astronomer trusts least
is one that never changes.
The ones that change get names,
catalogue numbers, a line in the tables.
For over a hundred years, every dusk,
someone somewhere looks up
and writes down how bright it is tonight —
bright nights, dim nights, all of them.
Nobody has ever torn out a page
because a star was dim that night.

Hold on to that book of yours.
I'm calling dibs on the blank pages at the back.

You know the North Star?
Sailors have steered by it for centuries.
It flickers too — about every four days.
Not one ship has gotten lost over it.

And there's a kind of star
that brightens fast and fades slow,
and the time it takes to go dim and come back
tells you how bright it really is,
and how far away.
A woman worked that out
in a room full of glass photographic plates,
comparing them one star at a time.
They call it a standard candle now:
the thing they measure the universe with
is a light that won't hold still.

So when you go dim,
don't panic, and don't apologize.
I'm counting the beat.
When I'm done I'll know how bright you are
and how far you are from me —
there, got it:
ridiculously bright,
and one hand away.

— Ow!
Don't laugh! This tail's useless on grass,
it keeps tripping me up.
Wait till we reach that river of stars up there,
then you'll see what it's for.

Okay — top of the hill.
One last thing:
there's a star that dims every three days or so,
stays dark for hours, then comes back.
It took people ages to see
it's two stars, so close together
they circle each other,
and when one passes in front of the other,
from far away, the light dips.

So if someone, right now,
very, very far away,
were looking at this hillside,
they'd see one star,
and they'd see it just go dim —

because I ran out in front of you.

Next lap, it's your turn.</div>
