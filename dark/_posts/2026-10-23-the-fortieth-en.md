I've torn down thirty-nine castles.

The first took forty days. The thirty-ninth took a morning. Unbuilding a castle is like eating a fish: find the right bone, pull, and the whole thing comes apart.

Number forty sat on a hill above the river mouth. The garrison had surrendered the day before. They filed down the slope and laid their swords at my feet like homework.

"All gone?" I asked.

"All gone, sir," said Grimm, my second. "Just a few masons left."

"Run the masons off too."

"We did." Grimm wiped his forehead. "They climbed back up."

I shouldered my hammer and went up the hill.

At the east corner a girl as skinny as a broom was laying stone. Beside her sat an old man with one leg splinted to a plank and a chalk line between his teeth. "Crooked. Left. More left. There."

"Hey," I said.

The girl didn't turn around. "Wait your turn."

"I'm here to tear this castle down."

"I know." She smoothed a bed of mortar. "That's why you wait. We build, then you knock."

Forty-two years old, and nobody had ever told me to wait my turn.

I swung. One blow, and a stone the size of a millwheel dropped out of the east wall and bounced down the hill, scattering gulls.

She looked at the gap. Then she bent, hugged up a stone bigger than her own head, and shoved it back in.

"Mortar!" the old man shouted.

"Coming!"

I swung again. She set another.

Grimm yelled from below. "Sir, want some men up there?"

"No!" I roared. "Nobody comes up!"

This was my castle. I mean — the castle I was taking down.

By noon my arms were lead. The wall was shorter, but it was standing. Her fingers were split and wrapped in rags, and the rags had gone red. The old man was on his third chalk line.

"What's the point?" I panted. "Your garrison quit. Nobody wants this place."

"You do," she said. "You want it gone."

"You can't win. I've got three hundred men, twenty oxen, two trebuchets. You've got an old man with one leg and a—"

"And someone who builds faster than you break." She tapped a stone into a seam. "That last swing was off, by the way."

I looked. It was. The stone had cracked; the wall hadn't budged.

Twenty years of swinging, and I had never once missed.

I laughed. I couldn't stop. Down the hill, Grimm decided I'd lost my mind. Thirty-nine castles, and not one had ever hit back. They just fell. Politely. Boringly.

"All right." I switched the hammer to my left hand. "Come on."

<p class="ds-cas-cut">⚒</p>

The afternoon wind came off the sea, hard and salt. I had the trebuchets hauled up the hill. I'd said nobody comes up, but a trebuchet isn't a body.

The first shot punched through the gatehouse. She stood in the rubble, hugged up the keystone from the arch, and walked off with it.

"Where are you going?"

"Done with walls," she said without looking back. "You can knock walls down."

"Then what are you building?"

No answer. The old man hobbled after her on his plank, and the two of them crouched in the middle of the castle, where the courtyard had been, and began marking lines. Not a square. A point, with eight lines running out of it, like a star, or like —

I couldn't read it. I know every shape a castle comes in: square towers, round towers, turrets, moats. I know where their bones are.

This thing had no bones. It had one direction. Up.

"What is that?" I asked Grimm.

He squinted. "Doesn't look defensible. No arrow slits. No door."

"Then what's it for?"

"Maybe… for being tall?"

The second shot was loaded. The crew watched me, waiting for the nod.

She stood in the middle of her eight lines, set down the first stone, and looked up at me. Red hands, grey face, eyes like iron fresh out of the quench.

"Go on," she said. "You knock one down, I put one up. You die, I keep building. I die, someone else does. One day there'll be something on this hill so tall your hammer can't reach it."

I raised my hand.

Three hundred men held their breath. The gulls shut up. The wind stopped in midair.

I have never lost. I wasn't planning to start today.

But I wanted to see it.

I wanted to see it at ten courses. At a hundred. I wanted to know how high it could go.

My hand hung in the air, and before it came down —
