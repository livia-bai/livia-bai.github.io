Monsieur Aubrac set the lump of metal on the scale the way you would set down somebody else's heart.

"Four hundred and twelve grams." He pencilled the figure into Marthe's ledger, then copied it into his own little book. "One plate. Every scrap left over comes back to me. Every grain."

The stuff was greyish white, dull, and far lighter than a lump of tin that size. The first time Marthe picked it up her hand jumped. The paper it came in was stamped with a foreign word: ALUMÍNIO. Madame Aubrac had family in Brazil; this was her father's wedding gift, and gram for gram it was worth more than gold. On Thursday a painter was coming to paint the whole family at dinner, and Monsieur wanted this plate in the middle of the table with the pewter ranged around it.

"Can a pewterer manage it?"

"I've worked pewter twenty years," said Marthe. What she didn't say was that she had only ever seen this metal's name in the newspaper.

---

After closing, three knocks.

It was the Aubracs' eldest, Louis, fifteen, carrying a flat wine flask as if he'd just been sent out for a refill. He put a square of folded paper on the counter and left.

The note was in Madame Aubrac's hand:

*That metal was my father's gift to me, not to him. The day the painting is done he'll sell it to cover his gambling. Make the plate thin. Give what you shave off to Louis — enough to get him to Paris. He will weigh the scraps. That part is up to you. Burn this.*

Marthe fed the note to the furnace and watched it curl and blacken. Then she weighed the metal again.

Four hundred and twelve.

---

The casting went fine. The stuff melted long before copper would, poured into the sand mould, and set into a grey disc.

The trouble was the hammer.

Pewter is soft; it takes whatever you give it. This metal took a dozen blows and went hard, and a few more opened hairline cracks along the edge. It needed annealing — heating soft again before more hammering.

Marthe had apprenticed to a silversmith. Silver, when it's ready, glows a dull red; you see the red, you pull it out. She slid the disc into the coals and waited for red.

It never came.

The metal stayed exactly that greyish white, gave her no colour at all, and then one side simply sagged, like a candle running, and a drop rolled off into the coals.

When she got it out with the tongs, the blank was missing a corner and there was a bright little bead among the embers. She picked the bead out and put it on the scale.

Nineteen grams.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she wrapped it in paper and wrote an L on the paper.

A ruined piece can be said to have burned away. Nobody weighs a bucket of ash.

---

But she couldn't ruin it twice. The missing corner had to be made up by driving the rest of the metal outward; the plate would only get thinner, and one more slump would leave nothing.

Next morning, washing her hands, she noticed the bar of lavender soap on the bench — Madame Aubrac had left it last month when she came to order a pewter jug. Marthe drew a line of soap across the blank and pushed it back into the fire.

The soap bubbled. Then it yellowed. Then it went black.

The moment it went black she pulled the blank and struck it. Soft.

After that, every trip into the fire got a soap line, and black meant out. Under the hammer the blank spread ring by ring, grew shallow, raised its rim. After each ring she gave it one extra stroke of the file. The filings she swept into two places: the scrap jar, and the paper marked L.

Tuesday evening she did the sums, and they didn't come out. From the waste of her own pewter jugs she filed a pinch of tin dust, tipped it into the scrap jar, and stirred it in with a nail. Tin is more than twice as heavy as this stuff. Grey into grey: no one could tell.

---

On Wednesday at noon Monsieur Aubrac came himself.

He didn't look at the plate first. He looked at the scrap jar. Jar in one pan, plate in the other; out came the little book, and his lips moved as he added.

Marthe stood behind the counter with her hands in her apron pocket. There was nothing in the pocket. She just didn't know where else to put them.

"Plate, three sixty-four. Scraps, forty-one." He looked up. "Four hundred and five. Where are the other seven?"

"The fire," said Marthe. "It doesn't change colour before it melts. I nearly lost the whole plate." She nudged the ash bucket toward his shoes. "It's all in there, if you want it."

He looked at the bucket of black ash and didn't reach for it.

He lifted the plate on one finger, weighed it, and smiled.

"Light as a leaf."

"Yes."

He wrapped the plate in velvet, then tucked the scrap jar under his arm as well. "They'll sell together," he said.

---

Louis came back toward evening, with the same flask. Marthe pushed the paper packet into the inside pocket of his coat.

"Thirty-one grams. Enough to get you to Paris. Not enough to get you back."

The boy nodded, uncorked the flask, took a swig, and choked until his face went red — it really was wine. At the door he turned. "Papa says the painter will put the plate right in the middle."

"In paint it'll be the same colour as the pewter," said Marthe. "Grey."

The door shut. She began to sweep, got as far as the furnace, and stopped.

Only one man in town bought this metal: Bertin, on the east side. Bertin kept a scale far finer than Monsieur Aubrac's, and a small bottle of acid. Tin dust dropped in that acid fizzes differently.

She leaned the broom against the wall and took a good bottle of brandy from the bottom of the cupboard. Bertin owed her a favour, and the price of a pewter jug.

Monsieur Aubrac wouldn't sell until the painter had gone. She had to get there first.
