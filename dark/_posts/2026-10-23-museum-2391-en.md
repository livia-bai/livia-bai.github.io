<p class="ds-mu-intro">Welcome to the Museum of the Early Screen Age, Gallery 7: <b>Small Things</b>.<br>All objects in this hall were recovered from ordinary homes, circa 2026. Labels are written by our research staff. If any of them are wrong, please say so in the guestbook. We would genuinely like to know.</p>

<div class="ds-mu-label"><span class="ds-mu-no">Exhibit 01</span>
<h3>Wrist Loop (Elastic)</h3>
<span class="ds-mu-meta">c. 2026 · rubber, fabric</span>
<p>Almost always recovered on the owner’s wrist rather than in their hair. The leading theory is that it served as a reminder, though of what has been lost. Period sources repeatedly record a single anguished cry: <i>“Where is my hair tie?”</i> At the moment of the cry, the hair tie was typically on the wrist of the person crying it.</p></div>
</section>

<div class="ds-mu-label"><span class="ds-mu-no">Exhibit 02</span>
<h3>Receipt (Thermal Paper)</h3>
<span class="ds-mu-meta">c. 2026 · paper</span>
<p>The most common document of the era, and nearly every surviving example has faded to blank. Scholars have argued for over a century about why a civilization would keep its accounts in disappearing ink. One school calls it a technical flaw. The other calls it humility: the things you bought need not be remembered for long. The museum has adopted the second view, on the grounds that it is nicer.</p></div>
</section>

<div class="ds-mu-label"><span class="ds-mu-no">Exhibit 03</span>
<h3>Sock (Single)</h3>
<span class="ds-mu-meta">c. 2026 · cotton</span>
<p>Socks left the factory in pairs and, within months, became singles by a process not yet understood. Our collection holds more than eleven thousand single socks. No two have ever matched. The fate of the other one remains one of the three great mysteries of the period. The other two: why charging cables knot themselves precisely when needed, and Exhibit 05.</p></div>
</section>

<div class="ds-mu-label"><span class="ds-mu-no">Exhibit 04</span>
<h3>Disposable Chopsticks (Unopened, 41 Pairs)</h3>
<span class="ds-mu-meta">c. 2026 · bamboo, paper sleeves</span>
<p>Recovered from a single kitchen drawer, never used. Researchers believe the household was preparing for a very large dinner party: forty-one guests, and somehow never quite the right evening. We prefer to believe the weather was lovely the whole time, and everyone was just a little busy.</p></div>
</section>

<div class="ds-mu-label"><span class="ds-mu-no">Exhibit 05</span>
<h3>The Little Spinning Circle (Reconstruction)</h3>
<span class="ds-mu-meta">c. 2026 · light</span>
<p>Not an object, but a figure that appeared only on screens. Contemporaries reportedly stared at it for minutes each day, motionless, doing nothing at all. Some scholars call this meditation. Others call it prayer, the prayer being roughly <i>“come on.”</i> The reconstruction to the left has been spinning since the museum opened and has not yet loaded anything. Please do not rush it.</p></div>
</section>

<div class="ds-mu-label"><span class="ds-mu-no">Exhibit 06</span>
<h3>Keyring Charm (Plush, Bear)</h3>
<span class="ds-mu-meta">c. 2026 · polyester, metal</span>
<p>Four times the size of the keys it hung from. Opens no doors. And yet the wear pattern shows it was held in the palm far more often than the keys ever were. The researcher’s report ends with one unacademic line: <i>“Perhaps, back then, leaving the house took a little courage.”</i></p></div>
</section>

<div class="ds-mu-label"><span class="ds-mu-no">Exhibit 07</span>
<h3>Sticky Note (Yellow)</h3>
<span class="ds-mu-meta">c. 2026 · paper, a little glue</span>
<p>Found on a refrigerator door. It bears a single word: <i>“Remember.”</i> Nothing follows. Remember the milk? The electricity bill? To call someone back? Something else entirely? There is no way to know. Our staff debated the matter for forty years and finally agreed, unanimously, not to fill it in.</p></div>
</section>
