Thursday noon, there were two signs on the pole at the crossing, the top one green, the bottom one red. Old Gus the firewood man's mule, Pepper, died underneath them.

The bottom sign is Mrs. Ottavia's. The top one went up on Monday.

<p class="ds-bob-lace" aria-hidden="true"></p>

On Monday a Mr. Pratt arrived from the Hollow Tooth Mine's head office back East, with a wooden crate. Stenciled on the side in white: NO. 8 AUTOMATIC CROSSING SIGNAL.

He stood in the crossing and talked to the sheriff in a voice meant for the whole street.

"The Seven still needed a man to wind it. The Eight doesn't. The ore cars wind it. The Nine comes out next year, and it won't even need the cars."

He buried a plate under the mine rails. Every car that rolled over it went *clank*, and the spring in the box took one more turn. The spring drove a little fan; however fast the fan spun, that's how fast the sign flipped.

"More wind, it runs faster, doesn't it?" said the sheriff.

"Which is why it's twelve cars a day." Pratt patted the box. "Exactly one day's worth."

The box went up the pole a foot above her sign, with a second sign hanging under it, red one side, green the other. Pratt climbed a ladder to set the time, and the book he was reading from was the deputy's little notebook.

I nearly forgot to turn. That deputy sat on the bench three days, clicking his watch every time the sign flipped. I thought he was trying to catch her.

He was copying her.

"Ore drivers watch the top sign from today. Company orders." Pratt came down the ladder. "Townsfolk can watch whichever they like. We run it a week. If it holds, the booth comes down."

Nobody came in to tell her. She hummed, and watched the pins.

"Old Bone! It's going to replace her!"

"Cross," said Old Bone. I crossed. "A sign that's right is a sign that's right."

<p class="ds-bob-lace" aria-hidden="true"></p>

Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, the two signs flipped together.

Not a hair apart. The red dot slid under my belly, her hand found the rope, and up on the pole the box went *click*, and both signs turned at once, like two people opening their mouths to say the same word.

I couldn't tell you what was frightening about it. It didn't do a single thing wrong.

People started craning up at the top sign. The ore drivers first, then the mail rider, the woman with the egg basket, and at last even the feed-store man. Only old Gus still watched the bottom one. He led Pepper into town every noon with a load of firewood, and he said he didn't trust anything that didn't eat.

Wednesday, just before quitting time, a boy came running down from the mine and shouted at the bench from under the pole: "Starting tomorrow, sixteen cars a run!"

Pratt, on the bench, nodded. He didn't ask why.

<p class="ds-bob-lace" aria-hidden="true"></p>

Thursday's first run, *clank, clank, clank*: I counted to twelve and the plate was still going. Sixteen.

The hum in the box went thin and high, like a mosquito coming closer.

The first time it was a hair: the top sign clicked, and then her hand found the rope. The next time, one cross. The time after that, a cross and a twist.

The top sign was talking over her.

"Old Bone, it's fast!"

Old Bone didn't say cross. Old Bone said, "She isn't slow."

"But the ore drivers watch the top one!"

"I know."

<p class="ds-bob-lace" aria-hidden="true"></p>

By noon the top sign was most of an eye ahead.

Down this end of the street, the bottom sign was still green. Old Gus led Pepper and a wagon of firewood into the crossing, one step at a time.

Up the hill, the top sign had already gone green. The first car came over the grade.

The brakeman saw the mule. He hauled the brake till he was hanging off the lever with his whole body. Sixteen cars: four cars heavier than usual.

Gus jumped clear. Pepper didn't.

I'm not writing that sound.

In the booth, the red dot arrived. Her hand found the rope, and pulled, and the bottom sign turned red.

Top green, bottom red. That's how.

<p class="ds-bob-lace" aria-hidden="true"></p>

The mine men came down the hill, each with a pick handle carried upright, and all at once the crossing bristled with poles, like the pins on her pillow. Pratt rode through them on a white horse. He'd been up at the mine all morning.

Gus stood beside the mule with no shirt on. The shirt was over Pepper's head. Two dogs nosed under the scattered firewood.

Pratt looked up at the two signs, took out his watch, and compared for a long time.

"The bottom one's slow."

Every face turned to the booth.

"She's not slow!" I shouted from the pillow. "It's fast! Sixteen cars! You *nodded*!"

Nobody can hear a bobbin.

Pratt climbed the ladder, opened the box, and reached in with two fingers and turned something. The hum shot up into a whistle, spun a good long while, and stopped. The spring had run all the way down.

He shut the box. "There. Adjusted."

"Third screw from the left," said Old Bone.

"What?"

"He turned the third screw from the left. Remember it."

Old Bone has never in his life talked about anything but stitches.

<p class="ds-bob-lace" aria-hidden="true"></p>

Pratt walked around the outside of the booth, took out a pocketknife, cut the rope that ran from our window up to the pole, and tied the loose end off on a nail.

The sheriff watched from his horse and didn't stop him. He looked at the booth, then at Pratt. He looked at Pratt longer.

The next red dot arrived. Her hand found the rope, and pulled.

The rope was slack. A long length of it came in and piled in her lap.

Her song missed half a beat.

Then she hummed on. The next red dot arrived, and her hand found that length of rope again, and pulled. Nothing.

The top sign flipped by itself.
