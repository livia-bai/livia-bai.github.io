This place is called White Pigeon Nest. I am grey.

My father was grey. My mother was grey. My family has been grey on these eaves for more generations than anyone has counted. The name belongs to the birds of a rich Portuguese merchant from two hundred-odd years ago, who kept a whole roofline of white ones. They lived here a few years and left, and what they left was a name. We've lived here two hundred years, and what we've left is mostly on the pavement.

At noon there was a wedding at St Anthony's, and they let ten white ones go. Nine circled the church once and headed north. I know where: two streets over, a rooftop with a tin-sheet loft. I fly over it every day.

The tenth came down in the square, on Mrs Ho's junk stall.

Mrs Ho has a bowl on her stall the size of a mandarin. White porcelain, blue curlicues, red flowers. It had a lid once. The lid got lost, nobody would buy it without one, so she fills it with water and keeps it on the corner of the stall. Three years now, that's been my water.

The first thing it did when it landed was put its beak in my bowl.

A tourist took its picture. Then another. Then an entire tour group. White bird, white bowl, blue curlicues, red flowers, so pretty it looked fake. I stood right beside it for ten minutes with half a thread wound round my foot, and I didn't get into one photo. A kid threw bread. Over my head. To it.

At half past one a young man ran up carrying an empty cage. His vest said MAGPIE WEDDINGS, and it was soaked front and back.

"Ice Cream!"

It even had a name. It ignored him.

"It's mine," he told Mrs Ho. "I'm Lok. My number's on the leg ring."

"It flew here by itself," said Mrs Ho.

"It hatched on my roof. It eats the seed I mix. It has never found a single grain on its own in its life. You've got cats here. Kites. Buses. A pigeon that can't get home doesn't last a week."

"You let it go a dozen times a month," said Mrs Ho, "and every time you get people to photograph it flying. Today it actually flew, and now you're telling me it can't."

"It's lost!"

"Lost birds go round and round. It didn't go round. It came straight down, here." Mrs Ho tipped her chin at the eaves. "Two hundred years ago this whole row was white."

"Those were somebody else's birds! Its home is my roof!"

Neither of them reached for it. Reach, and it flies, and who knows where. Lok opened the cage door, set the cage beside the bowl, and went and sat on the bench. Mrs Ho sat back down on her stool. Ice Cream stood between them, looking at the cage, at the bowl, at the two of them.

Which of them is right, I couldn't tell you.

But I know both roads. What time the tart shop tips its broken pastry into the back lane, which tap outside the public toilet doesn't shut, which cat is blind: I know. The way back to that roof, two streets, drop at the tin: I know that too. If it followed me, it could go either way.

I'm not taking it either way.

Let it be a white pigeon in White Pigeon Nest for a few days. Let's see how many days it lasts.

I'm going for a drink. The two of them are waiting, Ice Cream is watching them wait, and for once nobody pecks me off.
