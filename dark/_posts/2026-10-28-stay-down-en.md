---

"Sixty-one."

Xu Tang had her face to the case glass, reading the hygrometer. The typhoon had been at it all afternoon. The power had been out for forty minutes, and the climate unit had stopped humming long before that.

I am a sheet of vellum: folio 146 recto of the Gotha Missal. Two columns of black, a few lines of red, a few small letters laid in gold. Paris, around 1375. Made for whom? Can't be proved. Before I was a book, I was a calf.

"Sixty-two."

Six hundred and fifty years. I had forgotten the calf completely. But when the air turns damp, the skin knows before I do. My lower left corner lifted. Like a leg.

"Do not open the case." On speakerphone, the loan courier, Ms Gardner, kept drowning under airport announcements. Flight cancelled, roads underwater; she couldn't get here. "Clause eleven. No one opens the case without the courier present. No one."

"Sixty-three."

My upper right corner lifted too. Something in me was waking: four legs, a wet nose, a hot shove upward that made me want to bellow.

But if I stand up, the gold comes off. The ink cracks. The red lines pucker.

I had to stay down.

Auntie Mei from cleaning wheeled her cart in. On it sat a bulging plastic bag. "From the staff-room drawer. Seaweed snacks, crackers, new shoes. The little packets. I've been saving them three years."

"We can't open the case," said Xu Tang.

"Who's opening the case?" Uncle Bing the electrician came in behind Mei, soaked to the thigh, a car battery on his shoulder: he had waded into the flooded basement car park and pulled it out of his own car. He lay down flat on the floor and unscrewed the access hatch in the base of the case. Inside was the drawer for the conditioning silica, separated from the glass above by a perforated board.

"I read clause eleven," he told the speakerphone. "It says glass."

Two seconds of silence. "...The drawer doesn't count."

Mei tore the packets open and poured. Hundreds of white beads hissed into the drawer. Bing wired the little fan in the base to the battery; it spun up, and air came under me, through the beads, and round again.

"Sixty-five."

"Everybody out!" Xu Tang shouted. "Breath is water!"

They backed out. Mei got as far as the door, came back, and emptied the last handful into the drawer, red in the face from not breathing. Xu Tang went last. At the door she stopped: somebody had to watch the number. She took one huge breath, held it, and pressed her face back to the glass, cheeks puffed out.

The hygrometer sat right beside me. Sixty-six.

All four corners were up. The calf was halfway to its feet inside me, knees braced, nose reaching. It wanted to run. It wanted the wet grass of six hundred and fifty years ago. I very nearly went with it.

Down.

Down!

Stay down!

Sixty-six.

Sixty-six.

Xu Tang went from red to purple.

Sixty-five.

She blew the whole breath out against the glass, clapped both hands over her mouth, and ran out of the room backwards.

Sixty-three. Sixty. Fifty-eight.

Out in the corridor, three people sat in a row on the wet floor, gasping.

The calf knelt back down, one leg at a time. It laid its head on its forelegs, rolled onto its side, and lay flat. My corners settled, one by one.

The gold was still there. So was the ink.
