### 7 December, 9:40 a.m., by the River Wear

Let me be clear: I never wrote *my son discovered*. I wrote *Oliver discovered*. There is a world of difference, and not one of the sixty-four recipients could see it.

The fog was thick enough to stand a spoon in. The cathedral across the river was a grey suggestion, its tower paler than the treetops, while its reflection in the water was perfectly crisp, as if the real cathedral grew on the riverbed and the one above was the copy. On the left, a row of bare trees, branches like hair nobody had brushed. On the right, one trunk wrapped root to crown in ivy, dark and bulky, standing there like a guest who won't take the hint.

Dennis jabbed his tripod into the mud and bent over his ancient camera. Dennis is Margaret's husband. In retirement he has taken up an old printing method called platinum printing, and at dinner last night he spent twenty minutes on it: platinum doesn't fade, a platinum print lasts five hundred years.

"Five hundred years," I said.

"At least," said Dennis. "Knocks silver into a cocked hat."

I looked at the patch of fog his lens was pointed at. In it were me, my son, my sister-in-law's husband, and the particular silence of people who have agreed, without discussion, not to be the first to mention last night. Five hundred years.

Oliver walked ahead, hands in his coat pockets, shoulders hunched. He's done that since he was small. When he's embarrassed he folds downwards, as if trying to become a size smaller.

I caught up and delivered the speech I'd been drafting all morning. "I've been thinking. I'll send everyone a short note. I'll call it 'A Small Addendum'—"

"Mum."

"—not a correction, a correction sounds like something *happened*. Just an addendum. A few technical details, that you're part of a team, and in a team, really, everyone discovers things together—"

"Mum. Professor Okafor emailed me this morning."

I stopped. A drop of water fell off the ivy tree, straight down the back of my collar.

"She wants me to do the school outreach talk in the spring," Oliver said. "On the helium cone. She said if I could survive last night, a room full of twelve-year-olds is nothing."

Somewhere in the fog, Dennis shouted, "Nobody move!"

I didn't move. I was thinking: next year's Christmas letter opens with this. I already had the headline.

### 6 December, 10:05 p.m., the Wear Room, Crown Hotel

If I could cut this part out of time, I'd sign the house over.

Picture it. Twenty-four people at the picture window, each with a pair of supermarket binoculars round their neck, all pointed at a wall of white where nothing could be seen. The cake half eaten. Me tapping a champagne flute with a spoon, louder than I meant to.

"Everyone!" I said. "In a few minutes, the Earth will officially pass through Oliver's Cone!"

Oliver stood by the dessert table, roughly the colour of the fog.

"Professor Okafor," I said, turning to the woman in the green jumper — she had actually come; her reply said she lived in town and would be honoured — "you're the expert. Could you show everyone which direction it's in?"

Twenty-four pairs of binoculars swung towards her.

The professor put down her plate. Early fifties, round glasses, laugh lines that made her look kind, which made what followed worse.

"It's here," she said.

Everyone waited.

"In this room," she said. "Helium atoms drift in from outside the solar system, and the sun's gravity bends them round behind it into a cone, the way a lens bends light to a point. Every year, around now, the Earth passes straight through. At this moment they're going through this table, through the cake, through all of you. You can't see them. Binoculars won't help. It takes an instrument on a satellite, looking at an ultraviolet glow no eye can see."

Silence. Uncle Gerald lowered his binoculars like a man lowering a flag.

"So Oliver," I said, "discovered the—"

"Oliver wrote our timing code," the professor said. "The clock on the satellite drifts, a few milliseconds a day. Leave it for a year and we'd have put the cone in the wrong week. He made the clock stop lying. Of the twenty-three of us, he's the quietest, and the one I'd fight hardest to keep."

I opened my mouth. Honestly, that was a perfectly good ending. Anyone else's Christmas letter could have stopped right there.

Then Margaret spoke. She held her champagne at chin height, her voice sweet enough to pull into threads.

"So, Gilly. Which bit of the cone did he discover?"

I have replayed this a thousand times since. I could have laughed and said, "I got carried away." Four words. Very easy.

What I said was: "…The cone bit."

Uncle Gerald laughed out loud and turned it into a cough. Someone dropped their binoculars on the carpet with a thud. Oliver looked down, picked up the cake knife, and began on the second half of the cake. Piped across it in buttercream: OLIVER'S CONE. Underneath, an enormous chocolate-dipped ice-cream cone.

### 6 December, 8:00 p.m., same room

Oliver arrived last, straight back from a conference in Geneva, towing a suitcase, his scarf beaded with fog. First he saw the banner over the door:

**WELCOME! FLY THROUGH OLIVER'S CONE WITH US!**

Then he saw the cake. Then the twenty-four pairs of binoculars. Then me.

"Mum," he said. "What did you… send?"

I had prepared for this. He'd been at the conference for weeks, his inboxes buried; he wouldn't have read the letter I sent to his personal address. Which was good. People always say *how could you* in the face of a surprise. Five minutes later they say *thanks, Mum*.

"Just the little letter," I said. "Same as every year. Go and say hello to Professor Okafor, she's already here."

The colour left his face from the forehead down.

"She's *here*?"

"I invited her."

"How did you even get her—"

"It's on the paper," I said. "Front page, right under her name. You scientists have no sense of self-preservation."

I hung a pair of binoculars round his neck and straightened his scarf. He didn't pull away, but he didn't look at me either. He looked past my shoulder at the professor by the window, and she saw him and raised her glass. He took a step forward as if someone had pushed him from behind, stopped, and turned back to ask, very quietly:

"Did you write *discovered*?"

I said, "Go and say hello."

---

### 6 December, 4:00 p.m., the bakery

I'd ordered the cake over the phone. I was perfectly clear: *helium focusing cone*, a themed cake for twenty-four, iced with OLIVER'S CONE.

The girl at the counter had a ring through her nose, and she wheeled the cake out of the chiller with enormous pride. A half-metre ice-cream cone, chocolate shell, and planted in the cream on top, seven helium balloons, pink and blue.

"Helium," she said, pointing at the balloons. "Cone." She pointed at the cone.

I stood at the counter for three seconds. No time to remake it. Tell her it was wrong and she'd cry. And honestly, out of twenty-four relatives, how many knew what a helium focusing cone looked like? I'd only looked it up the week before myself. The picture was a blurry blue-violet smudge, like someone had breathed on a pane of glass.

"Perfect," I said.

I paid and left a five-pound tip. At the time I thought this made me very gracious.

### 2 December, 7:12 a.m., the kitchen

The professor replied. She replied to all.

I sat at the kitchen table with my laptop open, looking at the sixty-four addresses packed into the recipient field, and felt like I was standing in a supermarket in my dressing gown.

> Dear Mrs Pryce,
>
> Thank you for your warm letter, and for the kind invitation. Oliver is a valued member of our team of twenty-three, and his work on data timing was essential to this paper. The result belongs to the whole team, and to the many researchers over several decades whose work came before ours.
>
> I'd be delighted to attend on 6 December.
>
> Adaeze Okafor

I read it four times. The first time, I read *team of twenty-three* and went hot in the face. The second time, I read *essential*. The third time, I read only *delighted to attend*. By the fourth, I had decided it was a letter of commendation.

Ten minutes later, Margaret also hit reply all:

> How exciting!!! See you there x

Three exclamation marks. Margaret and I have been sisters-in-law for twenty-six years and she has never once used more than one. Three means she's laughing.

I thought about it. Then I hit reply all too.

> Professor, you're far too modest! The whole family can't wait to meet you! Oliver has loved the stars since he was tiny!!!!

Four exclamation marks. I had to win.

Only after I'd sent it did I remember that Oliver had never loved the stars. He loved taking alarm clocks apart.

### 1 December, evening, the study

I send my Christmas letter on the first of December. Twenty-two years without a miss. Last year's ran to three pages, with a group photo from the Lakes.

This year I hadn't chosen a photo yet. I was on my third draft.

Draft one:

> This year Oliver took part in a scientific paper about the distribution of helium in the solar system.

I read it back and deleted *took part in*. What does that even mean? Handing someone a spanner is taking part.

Draft two:

> This year Oliver played a key role in an important space discovery.

Better. But *played a key role* sounds like the sports pages.

That afternoon, Margaret's letter had arrived. Four pages, colour printed. Her daughter Imogen had been promoted and had gone on Radio 4 to talk about "the future of urban beekeeping", where "the presenter called her the most infectious guest in the programme's history". The last page was a photo of Imogen holding a hive, bees circling her head like a halo.

I looked at that photo for a long time.

Draft three:

> This year's big news: Oliver has discovered an enormous cone made of helium! Every December the whole Earth flies right through it — and this 6 December we're inviting the entire family to Durham to fly through Oliver's cone together, in person!

I stared at *Oliver has discovered*. I told myself: strictly speaking, this paper was the first time Oliver had ever heard of the cone, so for him, it *was* a discovery. Writing that logic down now, I'd like to bury my head in a flowerbed. That evening it seemed watertight.

I opened the PDF of the paper, found the email address under the corresponding author's name, and added it to the recipients. Manners, I told myself. One ought to say thank you.

I pressed send.

---

### 14 January, 3:00 p.m., on the phone

Oliver rarely rings of his own accord. When he did, I was choosing a not-too-warm phrase for Margaret's birthday card.

"Mum, the paper's out." His voice was a bit high, a bit quick, the way it was the day he took apart his first alarm clock and put it back together and it still ticked. "My name's on it."

"Which paper?"

"The helium focusing cone. Basically, helium atoms drift in from interstellar space, the sun's gravity bends them round behind it into a cone, and the Earth goes through it every December. We've measured it better than anyone before."

"Your name's on it."

"Fourteenth. Out of twenty-three."

"Fourteenth," I said. "Is that good?"

He paused.

"It means I was there," he said. "I did the timing. The clock on the satellite drifts and I made it stop. It's not very interesting to explain, but without it the whole cone's out by a week."

I picked up an old envelope and wrote on the back: *Oliver — cone — December*. I underlined *December* twice.

"Are you coming home for Christmas?"

"Probably not," he said. "I have to watch the data in early December. The cone."

"Then we'll come to you."

He laughed, the laugh that means I'm hopeless. "Mum, you can't see it. You'd come all that way and see nothing."

"If you can't see it," I said, "what's the use of it?"

"That's the interesting bit."

He said a few more things and I said *mm*, *mm*, eyes on the back of the envelope. When he hung up, I crossed out the dash between *Oliver* and *cone* and put in an apostrophe and an *s*. It became: *Oliver's cone*.

Then I picked the phone back up and dialled Margaret.

"Margaret," I said. "What are you lot doing for Christmas this year?"
