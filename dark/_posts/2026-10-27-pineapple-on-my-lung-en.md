"Nothing," said Dr Ferrando. "The CT's completely clean."

I stood up and kissed him. Left cheek, right cheek, back to the left. He's twenty-nine. He went red all the way down to his collar.

"Signor Pittaluga, please, sit—"

"I won't sit! I've been sitting for fifty-two years!"

I hugged the coat stand behind the door. I lifted his jar of tongue depressors over my head and shook it like a maraca. I thumped the left side of my chest and coughed: a perfectly ordinary cough, left over from last week's cold. I loved that cough.

"I need to explain why," the doctor said. "Please let me finish."

He clipped Friday's X-ray onto the light box. In the middle of the left lung, a white blot the size of a fist, with a few curly white lines flung out around it. On Friday he'd pointed at that blot and said "six centimetres," and "I have to be honest with you," and "we'll do a CT on Monday, but you should prepare yourself." He said all of it to the film. He never once looked at me. So I believed every word.

"Radiology tells me you wouldn't take your waistcoat off."

I looked down. There it was: white silk with a pattern you only see when the light slides across it, scrolls woven in gold thread, and over the left side of my chest a gold pineapple sitting in a gold cup. My father wore it at his wedding. I wore it on my first day of work. On Friday I wore it to the doctor's, for luck.

"The technician asked you to remove anything metal. You told her you had no metal on you."

"I didn't!"

"It's eighteenth-century Italian brocade," he said. "Their gold thread was real silver, gilded, wound round a silk core."

He unclipped the X-ray, walked over, and pressed it flat against my chest.

The white blot sat exactly on the pineapple.

I laughed so hard I had to crouch on the floor. I'd carried a pineapple on my chest all weekend, thinking it was going to eat me. I hugged the doctor's shins and laughed. He said, "Signor Pittaluga." I said, "Bruno!" He said, "Signor Bruno, I'm begging you—" and the door banged open.

Ornella stood there with a long garment bag in her arms. She'd been circling for a parking space for forty minutes, and the rain had got half her hair.

"Well?"

"It's a pineapple!"

She looked at me, at the doctor, at the X-ray. The doctor explained again. When he'd finished, she flung the bag onto the examination couch and yanked the zip open.

The shroud. The other length of brocade, the one nobody was ever allowed to cut. Great-grandfather's rule: the last person named Pittaluga gets buried in it. Ornella's children have their father's name, and I never had any. The last one is me.

On Saturday morning she sat in my kitchen with the scissors and asked me three times, "Really?" I said cut it. I wanted to see how it looked on me while I was still alive to look. She sewed for two days and two nights. Her right forefinger is wrapped in tape.

"The year my daughter got married, I begged Papa to let us make her a dress out of it," she said. "Papa said no, it's for the last one. Twenty years. And I finally got to cut it."

She jabbed the shroud with her taped finger. "Cut to your size. Your size *now*."

"I'll wear it! I'll wear it right now!"

"It's a shroud!"

"Then I'll be the best-dressed living man in Genoa!"

She smacked my arm, smacked it again, then grabbed me and cried into my shoulder, crying the way other people shout.

Over the top of her head, I saw Tina.

Tina lives across the landing. Has done for twenty-two years. She was standing in the corridor with two coats over her arm, hers and mine. She'd heard everything. My voice carries the length of a floor.

On Saturday evening I'd knocked on her door and told her I loved her, had loved her for twenty-two years, had never dared say it, and if I didn't say it now I never would. She listened, went back in for a cardigan, came across, sat down on my sofa and said, "All right. Three months."

Ornella let go of me and stepped aside. The doctor raised the X-ray in front of his face.

"So it's not three months any more," Tina said.

"It's not!"

"Three months is what I said yes to."

I opened my mouth and shut it again. It was the first thing all morning I couldn't be happy about.

"Thirty years," she said, "with a man who hugs doctors' legs in their offices. That I'll have to think about."

I started working out how long she'd need. A week? A month? And if she thought it all through and said no?

And then I was happy again. I couldn't stop it.

"Think! Think as long as you like! I've got time!"

Tina looked at me for a long moment, then threw me my coat. "Lunch first. That's not a yes."

"It's not!"

I followed her out. Behind me, Ornella stuffed the shroud back into its bag and zipped it shut with one loud rip.

"This is going at the very back of my wardrobe," she said. "And you listen to me. Don't you dare get fat."
